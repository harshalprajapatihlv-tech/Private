Profit is not cash — start here, or the whole chapter will feel like magic

This is the heart of the chapter. If you only remember one sentence, remember this: a business can make a handsome profit and still run out of cash — and it can make a loss and still have cash coming in. The Profit and Loss Account does not tell you what happened to the bank balance. The Cash Flow Statement does.

Meet Meera. She runs a small garment shop in Pune. At the year-end her accountant smiles and says, “Madam, you made a profit of ₹1,00,000 this year.” Meera opens her bank app. She expected the balance to have grown by about the same amount. It has not. She is confused, and a little angry. This chapter exists because of Meera.

Here is everything that happened in Meera’s year. We will walk through each item and ask two questions: Did this change profit? Did this change cash? Those two answers are often different. That difference is the whole subject.

5. Accounting logic

Profit measures performance using accrual accounting: you count a sale when you earn it, and an expense when you consume it — even if cash moves on a different day, or never moves (depreciation). Cash flow measures liquidity: what actually went into and out of the bank. Both are true. They answer different questions. The Cash Flow Statement is the bridge: it starts from the profit figure you already trust, and walks you, line by line, to the cash figure you can see in the bank.

11. Memory trick

Three moves, always, in this order: (1) Add back non-cash expenses (depreciation, written-off items). (2) Undo non-operating items that sat inside profit (profit/loss on sale, interest, dividend income) so they can be placed in investing or financing. (3) Squeeze working capital — stock, debtors, prepaid, creditors, outstanding — because extra current assets lock cash and extra current liabilities keep cash in the business. Then tax paid. That is operating cash. Then buy/sell the tools (investing). Then talk to owners and lenders (financing).

Connect the chapters

Chapter on the Profit and Loss Account told you whether the business earned. Chapter on the Balance Sheet told you what it owns and owes on a date. This chapter tells you where the cash came from and where it went during the period. The three statements are a set. A Cash Flow Statement that does not reconcile to the change in cash on the two Balance Sheets is simply wrong — the examiner will see it in ten seconds.

What is a Cash Flow Statement?

1. What is it? — Definition

A Cash Flow Statement (CFS) is a statement that shows the inflows (receipts) and outflows (payments) of cash and cash equivalents during an accounting period, classified into operating, investing and financing activities. It explains the movement from the opening cash balance to the closing cash balance. In India it is prepared as per AS-3 (Cash Flow Statements) or, for companies following Ind AS, Ind AS-7 (Statement of Cash Flows).

2. In simple words

Imagine a video of your bank account for the whole year, sorted into three folders: (A) money from running the shop, (B) money spent on or received from long-term assets, (C) money from owners and lenders. That video, printed as a statement, is the Cash Flow Statement. It does not replace the P&L. It sits beside it.

3. Why do we need it?

Profit can hide a cash crisis. A fast-growing firm that sells on credit, fills its godown, and buys a new machine can show a profit and still bounce a cheque. Lenders, owners and managers cannot wait until the cash actually runs out. They need a statement whose last line is the same number as the cash on the Balance Sheet — with every rupee of movement labelled.

4. Real-life example

A Mumbai trader earns ₹8,00,000 profit in a year of boom. Almost all of it is sitting in debtors (retail chains pay in 90 days) and in extra Diwali stock. He has also paid ₹5,00,000 for a delivery van. His bank account is tight. The P&L says “well done”. The CFS says “operating cash is weak; investing ate the rest; you need a working-capital loan or slower growth.” Both statements are useful. Only one of them would have stopped him bouncing the VAT payment.

5. Accounting logic

Accrual accounting records when the right to cash arises, not when cash arrives. To convert accrual profit into cash, we reverse every accrual and every non-cash charge, and we park non-operating cash in the activity that actually produced it. That conversion is the indirect method.

Need and importance of a Cash Flow Statement

1. What is it? — Definition

The need for a Cash Flow Statement arises because profit is an accrual figure. Users who care about liquidity, solvency and the quality of earnings cannot read those things from the P&L alone. The CFS is the statement that reports the generation and use of cash, classified by activity, so that users can judge whether the firm can pay its bills, repay loans, pay dividends and fund growth from its own operations.

2. In simple words

You need a CFS for the same reason you look at your bank SMS thread and not only at your salary slip. The salary slip is the P&L. The SMS thread is the cash flow. Both matter. They are not the same document.

3. Why do we need it?

Without a CFS: (i) a lender cannot see whether instalments will be paid from operations or from fresh borrowing; (ii) management cannot see that stock and debtors are swallowing cash; (iii) investors cannot see whether dividends are being paid from genuine operating cash or from selling the family silver (fixed assets) or from new loans; (iv) the P&L and Balance Sheet remain two unconnected photographs instead of a film of the year.

Legal and accounting status in India (exam points)

  • AS-3 Cash Flow Statements is the Accounting Standard for companies that are not on Ind AS. It requires a CFS classifying cash flows into operating, investing and financing, and permits both direct and indirect methods for operating cash (Indian MBA exams almost always want the indirect method).
  • Ind AS-7 Statement of Cash Flows is the Ind-AS version (aligned with IAS-7). The three-activity structure is the same. The main exam-relevant difference is that Ind AS-7 allows options for classifying interest and dividend; AS-3 is stricter. For MBA we teach the AS-3 classic classification unless the question names Ind AS-7 and asks for the option.
  • Companies Act, 2013: a Cash Flow Statement is part of the financial statements. It is mandatory for companies other than One Person Companies, small companies and dormant companies (they are exempt unless they choose to prepare it). Listed companies must prepare it (SEBI LODR + accounting standards). “Prescribed companies” in older exam language means companies that the Act / rules require to include a CFS.
  • The CFS complements the P&L and the Balance Sheet. It does not replace either. Schedule III presentation still starts with the Balance Sheet and Statement of Profit and Loss; the CFS is the third primary statement.
12. Exam-ready answer

A Cash Flow Statement is needed because the Profit and Loss Account is prepared on the accrual basis and therefore profit is not equal to cash. The CFS reports inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents, classified into operating, investing and financing activities, as required by AS-3 (or Ind AS-7). It is useful to management for liquidity planning, to lenders for assessing debt-servicing capacity, and to investors for judging the quality of earnings. Under the Companies Act, 2013 it is mandatory for companies other than OPC, small companies and dormant companies; listed companies must prepare it. It complements the P&L and the Balance Sheet by reconciling the opening and closing cash balances.

This is a 5–8 mark theory question. If it says “Prepare a Cash Flow Statement” / “indirect method” / “AS-3”, it is a practical question — go to the format and the workings, not the theory.

Meaning of cash and cash equivalents

1. What is it? — Definition

Cash comprises cash on hand and demand deposits with banks (cash at bank). Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and that are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. AS-3 says an investment normally qualifies only when it has a short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition.

2. In simple words

Cash equivalents are not “investments” in the ordinary sense. They are parking places for spare money that you can turn back into cash almost at once, without worrying that the price moved. A 60-day Treasury bill bought two months ago is a cash equivalent. Shares of Infosys, even if you plan to sell them tomorrow, are not — equity shares have price risk, so they are investing items, not cash.

3. Why do we need it?

The CFS tracks the movement of the firm’s most liquid resources. If we counted only the notes in the till, a firm could “improve” its cash by shifting money into a 30-day deposit and the statement would show a fake outflow. Treating cash and cash equivalents as one pool stops that game. The last line of the CFS must equal cash + cash equivalents on the Balance Sheet.

4. Real-life example

On 31 March a company holds: currency ₹20,000, current-account balance ₹1,80,000, a 91-day T-bill bought on 1 March (maturity 30 May) ₹50,000, and 1,000 equity shares bought as a temporary parking ₹3,00,000. Cash and cash equivalents = ₹20,000 + ₹1,80,000 + ₹50,000 = ₹2,50,000. The equity shares are investments, not cash equivalents, even though the treasurer thinks of them as spare money.

5. Accounting logic

“Known amount of cash” + “insignificant risk of value change” + “short maturity (≤ 3 months from acquisition)” is a three-part test. Fail any one part and the item is an investment (investing activity), not cash. Equity shares fail the risk test. A two-year bond bought yesterday fails the maturity test. A six-month FD bought four months ago also fails (remaining life is short, but AS-3 looks at maturity from the date of acquisition, not remaining life).

The three activities — the skeleton of every Cash Flow Statement

1. What is it? — Definition

AS-3 requires every cash flow to be classified into one of three activities. Operating activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of the enterprise and other activities that are not investing or financing. Investing activities are the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. Financing activities are activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the owners’ capital and borrowings of the enterprise.

2. In simple words

Operations = running the shop. Investing = buying and selling the shop’s tools (and spare long-term money). Financing = talking to owners and lenders. Every line you write on a CFS must sit in one of these three rooms. If you cannot decide which room, you have not yet understood the item.

3. Why do we need it?

Classification is the whole point. A firm that generates ₹1,00,00,000 from operations and spends it on a new plant is a different animal from a firm that generates ₹1,00,00,000 by issuing shares and using the money to cover an operating cash drain. The total change in cash can be identical. The story is not. Examiners award marks for putting the rupee in the correct activity, not merely for the final cash figure.

11. Memory trick

Shop / Tools / Owners-and-lenders. If cash moved because a customer, a supplier, an employee or the tax department was involved in day-to-day business → operating. If cash moved because a long-term asset or an investment was bought or sold, or because interest/dividend was received → investing (AS-3). If cash moved because shares, debentures or loans were issued or repaid, or because interest/dividend was paid → financing (AS-3).

Operating activities

1. What is it? — Definition

Operating cash flows are the cash effects of transactions that determine net profit or loss, other than those that are classified as investing or financing. In the indirect method, we do not list cash from customers and cash to suppliers. We start from profit and adjust it until only operating cash remains.

2. In simple words

If you closed the shutters, stopped buying machines, and neither borrowed nor repaid anyone — the cash that would still move is operating cash. Customers pay you. You pay suppliers, staff, rent, power, and income tax. That is the shop running.

4. Real-life example

A restaurant’s operating inflows: cash from diners, collections from credit-card companies. Operating outflows: vegetables, salaries, rent, gas, GST/income tax paid. Buying a new oven is not operating (investing). Taking a loan to buy the oven is not operating (financing). Paying the baker is operating.

Investing activities

1. What is it? — Definition

Investing activities are the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and of other investments not included in cash equivalents. They show how much cash was used to obtain resources that will generate future income and cash flows — and how much cash came back when those resources were sold.

2. In simple words

Buying or selling the things the business keeps (plant, buildings, patents, shares of other companies, long-term deposits) is investing. The cash that changes hands is the actual sale proceeds or the actual purchase price, not the book value, not the profit or loss on sale. Profit or loss on sale is only a P&L figure; we remove it from operating and put the real cash in investing.

4. Real-life example

A factory sells an old lathe. Cost ₹80,000, accumulated depreciation ₹50,000, book value ₹30,000, sold for ₹35,000 cash. Investing inflow is ₹35,000 — the proceeds — not ₹30,000 and not the profit of ₹5,000. The ₹5,000 profit is deducted from operating profit so it is not counted twice.

Financing activities

1. What is it? — Definition

Financing activities are those that result in changes in the size and composition of the owners’ capital (equity, including preference share capital) and borrowings of the enterprise. They answer: how did the firm raise cash from owners and lenders, and how did it return cash to them?

2. In simple words

If the other party to the cash movement is an owner (shareholder) or a lender (bank, debenture-holder), it is financing — with the AS-3 twist that interest paid and dividend paid are also financing, even though interest was an expense in the P&L.

4. Real-life example

A company issues 10,000 equity shares of ₹10 at a premium of ₹5. Cash received = 10,000 × ₹15 = ₹1,50,000. The whole ₹1,50,000 is a financing inflow (share capital ₹1,00,000 + securities premium ₹50,000). The split between capital and premium matters for the Balance Sheet; for the CFS it is one cash inflow: proceeds from issue of shares.

Formula

This is the default for MBA exams unless the question names Ind AS-7 and asks you to use an option.

Exam tip

The examiner’s favourite trick is to leave interest paid inside profit and then also forget it in financing — or the reverse, to show it in financing without adding it back in operating. Both errors understate or overstate total cash by the interest figure. The pair is compulsory: add back interest expense in operating, then show interest paid as a financing outflow. Same pairing for interest/dividend income: deduct in operating, show as investing inflow.

9. How to identify the question

If the question says “classify the following into operating, investing and financing”, write three headings and drop each item in the correct bucket using the AS-3 lists above. If it says “interest received” without naming Ind AS-7, it is investing. If it says “dividend paid”, it is financing. If it says “purchase of machinery by issue of shares”, write non-cash investing and financing transaction — disclosed in a note, not in the CFS body.

Indirect method — the format you must memorise

1. What is it? — Definition

Under the indirect method, net cash from operating activities is obtained by adjusting net profit before tax for non-cash items, unpaid/unearned items, non-operating items that belong in investing or financing, and changes in working capital, and then deducting income tax paid. Investing and financing sections then list the actual cash receipts and payments of those activities. Direct method (cash from customers, cash to suppliers) exists in AS-3 but is not the method this syllabus asks you to prepare.

2. In simple words

Indirect means: do not start from the cash book. Start from the profit you already computed. Then repair that profit until it becomes operating cash. It is called “indirect” because cash from customers is never shown as a line; it is buried inside “profit ± adjustments”.

3. Why do we need it?

Indian companies almost always publish the indirect method. MBA exams give you a P&L (or two Balance Sheets from which you reconstruct profit) plus additional information. That data set is designed for the indirect format. Learn one format, in this order, and never shuffle the blocks.

  1. Read the question and mark three things: the two cash figures (opening and closing), the profit figure (or the two P&L surplus balances from which you will reconstruct profit), and the additional information (depreciation, sales of assets, tax, dividend, interest).
  2. Reconstruct Net profit before tax if it is not given: Closing P&L surplus − Opening P&L surplus + dividend paid (and/or current proposed dividend if appropriated) + transfer to reserves + bonus issue from P&L = Profit after tax. Then PAT + current tax charge = PBT. (Full working is later in this chapter.)
  3. Start the operating section with that PBT. Add back every non-cash expense (depreciation, amortisation, written-off items, loss on sale). Deduct every non-cash / non-operating income (profit on sale, interest income, dividend income). Add back interest expense.
  4. You now have operating profit before working capital changes. For every current asset except cash: increase → deduct, decrease → add. For every current liability except items you are treating as financing (often bank overdraft) and except provision for tax / proposed dividend (those have their own workings): increase → add, decrease → deduct.
  5. This gives cash generated from operations. Deduct income tax paid (not the P&L tax charge). Result is (A).
  6. Build investing (B): actual cash paid for PPE and investments, actual sale proceeds, interest received, dividend received. Derive missing purchase / sale figures from Plant A/c and Accumulated Depreciation A/c.
  7. Build financing (C): cash proceeds of issues and loans, cash repayments, interest paid, dividend paid. Ignore bonus issues and conversions.
  8. Compute A + B + C. Add opening cash and cash equivalents. The total must equal closing cash and cash equivalents on the Balance Sheet. If it does not, a working is wrong — find it before you move on. This equality is the only full-statement check the examiner has, and it is the only full-statement check you have.
Key point

The last three lines are not decoration. A + B + C + Opening cash = Closing cash. If you cannot make this identity true, you have not finished the question. Do not “plug” the difference into some random line. Go back to plant, tax, dividend and working capital — that is where almost every missing rupee hides.

Working notes the examiner expects — how to find the missing figures

A typical exam question does not give you profit before tax, tax paid, purchases of plant, sale proceeds and depreciation in a neat list. It gives two Balance Sheets and four or five lines of additional information. You have to build T-accounts (or equations) to find the missing numbers. These workings carry marks of their own. Show them. Do not hide them inside the CFS.

Working 1 — Reconstruct profit before tax from P&L surplus

6. Format — Profit before tax from two Balance Sheets

Closing balance of Profit & Loss surplus (from BS)

Less: Opening balance of Profit & Loss surplus

= Increase in surplus during the year

Add: Dividend paid / proposed (if it was appropriated out of this year’s profit)

Add: Transfer to general reserve / other reserves

Add: Bonus issue capitalised from P&L

= Profit after tax (PAT)

Add: Current year’s tax charge (provision made this year)

= Profit before tax (PBT) — this is the first line of the CFS

If the question already gives “Net profit before tax”, skip this working and start the CFS with that figure. If it gives “Net profit” after tax, add the current tax charge to reach PBT. Always be sure whether the starting figure is before or after tax, and before or after interest. The standard format starts from profit before tax, after interest — which is why we then add interest back.

Working 2 — Plant (fixed asset) at cost, and purchases

Formula

Opening plant at cost + Purchases − Cost of plant sold = Closing plant at cost

Therefore Purchases = Closing cost − Opening cost + Cost of plant sold. If no sale is mentioned, Purchases = Closing − Opening (when the BS shows cost, not WDV).

Some Balance Sheets show plant at written-down value (after depreciation) with no separate accumulated-depreciation line. Then the equation is:

Formula

Opening WDV + Purchases − WDV of asset sold − Depreciation for the year = Closing WDV

WDV of asset sold = Cost sold − Accumulated depreciation on sold, which equals Sale proceeds − Profit + Loss.

Working 3 — Accumulated depreciation, and depreciation for the year

Formula

Opening accumulated depreciation + Depreciation charged this year − Accumulated depreciation on asset sold = Closing accumulated depreciation

Therefore Depreciation for the year = Closing accum. dep. − Opening accum. dep. + Accum. dep. on asset sold.

Working 4 — Sale proceeds of a fixed asset

Formula

Book value (WDV) of asset sold = Cost of asset sold − Accumulated depreciation on it
Sale proceeds = Book value + Profit on sale − Loss on sale

If the question gives proceeds, you can run the same equation backwards to find profit or loss, and then use that profit/loss in the operating adjustments.

5. Accounting logic

Why we never put book value in the CFS: book value is not cash. The buyer pays the agreed price. That price is the investing inflow. The difference between price and book value is a non-cash gain or loss which we park as an operating adjustment so that it does not contaminate operating cash and is not double-counted.

Working 5 — Tax paid

Formula

Opening provision for tax + Current year’s tax charge − Closing provision for tax = Tax paid

“Current year’s tax charge” is the amount debited to P&L (the provision made this year). It is not the same as tax paid, and it is not the same as the closing provision.

If the question states “tax paid ₹…”, use that figure directly as the operating outflow and use the T-account only to find the current-year charge (which you need to reconstruct PBT). If there is advance tax or TDS, it is part of tax paid — do not show it twice.

Working 6 — Dividend paid

Formula

When proposed dividend is a Balance-Sheet liability in both years:
Dividend paid = Opening proposed dividend + Interim dividend (if any) + Current proposed − Closing proposed

Often there is no interim and current proposed is unpaid, so Dividend paid = Opening proposed dividend. If the question simply says “dividend paid ₹…”, use that. Do not deduct proposed dividend inside operating profit.

Watch out

Modern Company Law / revised AS-4 / Ind AS: proposed dividend is not a liability until it is declared. Many current Balance Sheets therefore do not show proposed dividend. In that case the question will give “dividend paid” in additional information, and P&L surplus is before that appropriation or the appropriation is already reflected — follow the numbers you are given. Older MBA textbooks still show proposed dividend as a current liability. Both presentations appear in papers. Read the BS.

Working 7 — Interest paid / received

Interest on 10% debentures of ₹2,00,000 for a full year = ₹2,00,000 × 10/100 = ₹20,000. Always write the multiplication. If debentures were issued or redeemed during the year, the question will usually tell you the interest figure, or tell you the date so you can compute time-proportion. If it is silent, a reasonable exam assumption is: issued/redeemed at year-end, so interest is on the opening balance (for redemption) or on the opening balance only (for issue at year-end, new money carries no interest this year). State your assumption in a working note.

Exam tip

Outstanding interest is a current liability. If interest expense is ₹20,000 and outstanding interest rose by ₹2,000, then interest paid = ₹20,000 − ₹2,000 = ₹18,000. Put ₹20,000 as the add-back (the P&L figure) and ₹18,000 as the financing outflow (the cash). Do not also count the outstanding-interest movement inside the general “outstanding expenses” line or you will double-count. Cleanest method: keep interest outstanding out of WC, and compute interest paid from the interest T-account.

Key point

Take debtors net of provision, exactly as a typical Balance Sheet presents “Trade receivables”. Do not add back the provision charge as a separate non-cash item. Do not treat the provision as a separate current liability. The movement in net debtors already does the right job: a pure increase in the provision (gross debtors unchanged) reduces net debtors, which you add in working capital, which cancels the non-cash charge that reduced profit.

Solved example 5.1 · Easy

Sneha Stores — first full CFS from a short list of facts

Sneha Stores provides the following information for the year ended 31 March 2026. Prepare a Cash Flow Statement by the indirect method.

  • Depreciation: ₹20,000
  • Tax paid during the year: ₹25,000

  • Increase in creditors: ₹8,000
  • Purchase of furniture (cash): ₹40,000
  • Loan taken: ₹30,000
  • Opening cash and cash equivalents: ₹15,000

Closing cash is not given. You must find it. That is normal.

Only one item: furniture purchased for cash ₹40,000. Net cash from investing activities (B) = (₹40,000).

Step 3 — Financing activities (C)

Only one item: loan taken ₹30,000. Net cash from financing activities (C) = ₹30,000.

₹93,000 − ₹40,000 = ₹53,000.

₹53,000 + ₹30,000 = ₹83,000 net increase in cash.

How to identify the question, the mistakes that cost marks, and what to remember

9. How to identify the question

You are in this chapter when the question says any of: “Prepare a Cash Flow Statement”, “Cash Flow Statement as per AS-3”, “indirect method”, “from the following Balance Sheets prepare a CFS”, “classify the following into operating, investing and financing”, “need / importance of Cash Flow Statement”. Two-year Balance Sheets plus additional information about depreciation, sale of an asset, tax and dividend is the standard 15-mark practical. A short list of adjustments is the 8–10 mark practical. Theory on need, cash equivalents, or classification is 4–8 marks.

10. Common mistakes
  • Treating depreciation as a cash outflow. Depreciation never leaves the bank. It is added back. Writing it as an investing or operating payment is a full-mark loss on that line.
  • Not deducting profit on sale (or not adding loss on sale) in operating. Then showing the full sale proceeds in investing double-counts the gain (or understates cash by the loss). Always pair: adjust the P&L result of the sale in operating, put proceeds in investing.
  • Putting purchase of a fixed asset in operating because “we paid cash to run the business”. Buying the shop’s tools is investing, not operating.
  • Treating a bonus issue as a financing inflow. Bonus is non-cash. Same error: conversion of debentures into shares, or an asset bought by issuing shares. Exclude from A, B and C; disclose in a note.
  • Using the closing provision for tax as “tax paid”. Tax paid = opening provision + current charge − closing provision. The closing provision is what is still unpaid.
  • Forgetting the interest pair. Add back interest expense in operating, then show interest paid in financing. Doing only one of the two drops (or double-counts) cash by that amount. Same pairing for interest/dividend income with investing.
  • Calling “cash” only the notes in the till and forgetting cash at bank / cash equivalents. The last line must equal the BS figure for cash and cash equivalents.
  • Squeezing provision for tax or proposed dividend through working capital as if they were creditors. They have their own cash lines (tax paid, dividend paid). Putting them in both places is a double count.
  • Using book value as the sale proceeds. Proceeds = WDV + profit − loss. Investing inflow is proceeds.
  • Taking the movement in plant at cost as the purchase, ignoring a sale. Purchases = closing cost − opening cost + cost of assets sold. Ignoring the sale understates purchases and therefore understates the investing outflow.
  • Mixing gross debtors with a separate provision, and also adding the provision charge back. Pick net debtors as per BS and stop. Do not also add back the provision.
  • Plugging the difference so that closing cash magically appears. If A+B+C does not equal the change in cash, a working is wrong. Find it.
11. Memory trick

Operations = running the shop; Investing = buying and selling the shop’s tools; Financing = talking to owners and lenders. Then the three repairs of the indirect method: add back non-cash, undo non-operating, squeeze working capital. CA up → cash down. CL up → cash up. Last line is a lock: A + B + C + opening cash = closing cash. AS-3 interest/dividend rhyme: received → investing; paid → financing.

Theory questions the examiner actually sets

Practical questions are the bulk of the marks. These theory questions still appear, usually for 4–8 marks. Answer in the shape the examiner rewards: definition, then three or four sharp points, then the standard tag (AS-3 / Companies Act) if it is relevant.

6 marks

Why is profit not equal to cash? Illustrate.

Profit is computed on the accrual basis. Revenue is recognised when earned and expenses when incurred, whether or not cash has moved. Cash is recognised only when it is received or paid. Therefore:

  • Credit sales increase profit but not cash until collected.
  • Depreciation decreases profit but not cash.
  • Purchase of a machine decreases cash but not profit (except later, through depreciation).
  • A loan increases cash but not profit.

Illustration: Meera’s profit ₹1,00,000 against a cash increase of only ₹55,000 in the opening example of this chapter — the gap is exactly these four kinds of item.

8 marks

State the importance of a Cash Flow Statement.

A CFS reports inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents, classified into operating, investing and financing activities (AS-3 / Ind AS-7). It is important because:

  • It reveals liquidity — the ability to pay creditors, lenders, employees and tax — which profit conceals.
  • Management uses it to plan working capital, capex and dividends.
  • Lenders use it to judge whether debt can be serviced from operations.
  • Investors use it to test the quality of earnings (is profit turning into cash?).
  • It complements the P&L and the Balance Sheet by reconciling opening and closing cash.
  • It is mandatory for listed companies and for companies other than OPC, small companies and dormant companies.
5 marks

What are cash and cash equivalents? Is a bank overdraft a cash equivalent?

Cash = cash on hand + demand deposits with banks. Cash equivalents = short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible into known amounts of cash and subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value, normally with a maturity of three months or less from the date of acquisition. Equity shares are not cash equivalents.

Bank overdraft repayable on demand may be treated as a component of cash and cash equivalents under AS-3 if it is an integral part of cash management. Many examination problems instead treat the movement in overdraft as a financing inflow or outflow. Follow the question.

8 marks

Distinguish operating, investing and financing activities, with two examples of each. How are interest and dividend classified under AS-3?

Operating — principal revenue-producing activities: cash from customers, cash to suppliers, wages, income tax paid.

Investing — acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and of investments other than cash equivalents: purchase/sale of plant, purchase/sale of investments.

Financing — changes in owners’ capital and borrowings: issue of shares, loans taken, redemption of debentures, repayment of loans.

AS-3 classification of interest and dividend: interest received and dividend received = investing inflows; interest paid and dividend paid = financing outflows. (Ind AS-7 permits options; unless named, use AS-3.)

Write the boxed format taught in this chapter, in this order: net profit before tax; adjustments for depreciation, profit/loss on sale, interest, non-cash items; operating profit before working capital changes; changes in current assets and current liabilities; cash generated from operations; tax paid; net operating (A); investing receipts and payments (B); financing receipts and payments (C); A+B+C; opening cash; closing cash. 6 marks

How will you treat the following in a Cash Flow Statement (indirect method, AS-3): depreciation; profit on sale of machine; bonus issue; increase in debtors; proposed dividend; purchase of machinery by issue of shares?

  • Depreciation — add back to profit (operating adjustment; non-cash).
  • Profit on sale of machine — deduct from profit; show sale proceeds as investing inflow.
  • Bonus issue — non-cash; exclude from the CFS body; disclose in a note. If capitalised from P&L, add back when reconstructing PAT.
  • Increase in debtors — deduct in working-capital adjustments (operating).
  • Proposed dividend — not an operating item. Dividend paid is a financing outflow (usually equal to opening proposed dividend if that is the unpaid liability brought forward).
  • Purchase of machinery by issue of shares — non-cash investing and financing transaction; disclose in a note; no figure in A, B or C.
